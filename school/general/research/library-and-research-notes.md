## 1. ⚠️ CAU AI policy (read this one first)
([screenshot](images/2026-10-12-ai-policy.webp))

- Generative AI tools like ChatGPT **should not be used for academic purposes.**
- **CAU considers the use of AI tools plagiarism** and a serious violation of academic integrity.
- Using another's ideas or words as your own includes ChatGPT, which is **not** your original thoughts or words.
- **Papers or assignments written by AI are plagiarized and get no credit.**
- It's **your responsibility to prove** your paper wasn't written with AI: keep pre-writing material such as related research, cited resources, outlines and drafts.
- The internet and AI are fine for finding **key terms and topics**, but they can be biased or promote an agenda. Stick to **.gov and .edu** sites for vetted information.
- If you do use AI: **explain why**, keep notes, and **save your prompts and the responses** in a document faculty can review.
- Faculty are "very adept at identifying when AI has been used."

**Our ground rules (agreed Oct 12):** this chat is for **studying, explaining, grading, correcting and the like. It is never for writing your papers or assignments.** You write your own work.

**How this applies to our chat:** I'm an AI, so keep it to studying: explaining concepts, quizzing you, organizing your notes. **Never submit anything I wrote as your own work.** Because the rules are strict, ask the librarian or your instructors whether using AI for study help (explanations, practice questions) is OK.

## 2. LIRN databases vs. the internet
([slide](images/2026-10-12-lirn-vs-internet.jpg))

| | **CAU LIRN databases** | **Internet** |
|---|---|---|
| Quality | Peer reviewed, so articles have more authority | Varies by site type (.com, .net, .gov, .edu, .org). Anyone can publish and info is hard to verify |
| Cost | Free to you (provided by CAU; sign in through Canvas) | Free with internet access, but some articles cost a fee (newspapers, journals) |
| Format | Full-text options. Organized by subject, collection and format | Each search engine returns different results, so it's hard to filter |
| Upkeep | Updated regularly | Not regularly reviewed or updated. Sometimes disappears |
| Best for | Quality research sources. Finding more resources through the references inside articles | Subject development, key words and ideas (like an encyclopedia or AI for keywords) |
| Watch out | Can be harder to use than the web, so give yourself time | |

**Hint:** Gale has a **Topic Finder** tool. Use it to narrow your search by subject or terminology.

## 3. The CRAAP test (verify your source)
([slide](images/2026-10-12-craap-test.jpg))

| Letter | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| **C**urrency | When was it published? Is it current enough to be relevant? |
| **R**elevance | Does it answer your research questions at an appropriate level? |
| **A**uthority | Who is the author, publisher or source? What are their credentials and experience? Are they qualified on this topic? |
| **A**ccuracy | Is it supported by evidence? Can you verify it in other reputable sources? Has it been reviewed or refereed? |
| **P**urpose | Why was it created: inform, persuade, sell or entertain? Is the author's point of view objective or biased? |

## 4. Peer-reviewed (scholarly) sources
- Think of why you trust a doctor or lawyer: education, experience, accreditation, training.
- Peer-reviewed articles are **prepared by professionals** in the field, **reviewed by experts**, and the experts decide whether the article is fit to publish.
- Using them "lends its expertise, knowledge and reputation to your paper and its claims." **Remember to cite!**
- **Why use databases:** reputable, reliable, evaluated information with no subscription. You can find news, magazines, scholarly peer-reviewed articles and books.

## 7. Search strategies
- Check **Full-Text**. For scholarly full-text works, also check **Peer-Reviewed**.
- Use **Advanced Search** with limiters: language (English), type, year.
- **Boolean operators:**

| Operator | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **AND** | Narrows | aircraft AND corrosion |
| **OR** | Broadens | corrosion OR rust |
| **NOT** | Excludes | corrosion NOT automotive |
| **" "** | Exact phrase | "weight and balance" |

- **Gale:** use *Publication Subject*. **ProQuest:** Advanced Search > *Thesaurus* to find terms.

## 8. The research process
(Full handout: [The Research Process PDF](resources/The-Research-Process.pdf))

1. **Great research takes time and effort.** Topics can cross many subjects.
2. **Think critically about your topic.** Narrow it to specific questions. For an assignment, use the prompts to refine your thesis.
3. **Keep a record of every source** (article, website, textbook, blog). You need it for your **APA reference page and in-text citations.** Make a Word doc named for your topic, and paste citations from the databases straight in.
4. **If the subject is new:** learn the basics from encyclopedias, textbooks and credible sources (.edu, .gov, LIRN databases, especially **Gale in Context** or the **Gale Topic Finder**).
5. **Narrow the question** using who, what, why, when, where and how. (Example: "What happens in a nuclear fallout?" becomes "What happened to wildlife after Chernobyl and Fukushima?")
6. Use a **thesaurus** to broaden search words. List key words and the subject areas your topic touches.
7. **Focus:** use up-to-date trade and scholarly sources. **Avoid Wikipedia.** Stick to .edu and .gov, and justify any commercial or organization sites. Textbook references in the back lead to more sources.
8. **Evaluate sources:** who is the author and what are their credentials? Any bias? Who's the audience? Opinion or objective? Does it compare viewpoints? What's the purpose (educate, inform, persuade, influence)? Does it help answer your question or support your thesis?
