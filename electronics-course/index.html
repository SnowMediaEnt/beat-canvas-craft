Lesson 01

Charge, current, voltage & power

Every circuit problem you will ever solve reduces to three quantities and two laws. This lesson rebuilds them from the ground up.

Charge and current

Electric charge is a property of matter, carried in circuits by electrons. It is measured in coulombs (C) — one coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 1018 electrons.

Current is charge in motion: the rate at which charge flows past a point. One ampere (A) is one coulomb per second. By convention, circuit diagrams show conventional current flowing from the + terminal of a source, around the circuit, and back to the − terminal — even though the electrons physically drift the opposite way. Every formula in this course uses conventional current, so you never have to think about electron direction again.

Voltage

Voltage is the electrical pressure that pushes charge around: formally, the difference in potential energy per unit of charge between two points, measured in volts (V). The water analogy holds up well: voltage is water pressure, current is flow rate, and resistance is a narrow section of pipe. Two things follow from it that trip people up:

  • Voltage is always between two points. "The voltage at this node" secretly means "relative to ground."
  • A battery can present voltage with no current flowing — like a pressurized tank with the valve shut.

Resistance and Ohm's law

Resistance is opposition to current flow, measured in ohms (Ω). The three quantities are locked together by Ohm's law, the single most-used equation in electronics:

V = I × R ↔ I = V / R ↔ R = V / I

Given any two of voltage, current, and resistance, the third is fixed. A 100 Ω resistor with 1 A through it must have 100 V across it; put 10 V across it and exactly 0.1 A flows.

Power

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is converted to heat, light, or motion, measured in watts (W). Combine P = V × I with Ohm's law and you get three interchangeable forms:

P = V I = I²R = V²/R

The squared terms matter: double the current through a resistor and it dissipates four times the heat. That is why undersized wiring and under-rated resistors burn, and why power calculations are a safety check, not an afterthought.

Units and prefixes

Real circuit values span enormous ranges, so metric prefixes do the heavy lifting. You will constantly convert between these:

Worked example

A pitot-heat element on a light aircraft draws 3.5 A from the 28 V bus. What is its resistance, and how much power does it dissipate?

R = V / I = 28 / 3.5 = 8 Ω
P = V × I = 28 × 3.5 = 98 W

Nearly 100 W of deliberate heating — which is exactly the point of pitot heat.

Lesson 02

Resistors & the color code

The most common component in electronics exists to do one job on purpose: oppose current. Here is how to read one, size one, and not burn one.

What resistors are for

A resistor converts electrical energy to heat at a controlled rate. That sounds wasteful, but it is how circuits limit current into LEDs and sensors, divide voltages down to safe levels, set gain and timing in amplifiers, and pull signal lines to a known state. In schematics you will see two symbols for the same part:

Reading the four-band color code

Through-hole resistors carry their value as painted bands. On the common four-band part: the first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier (the number of zeros to append), and the fourth is tolerance. Read with the tolerance band — usually gold or silver, and set apart by a gap — on the right.

Tolerance

Tolerance is the manufacturer's guarantee band. A 1 kΩ ±5% part may measure anywhere from 950 Ω to 1050 Ω and still be in spec. Precision circuits — instrumentation, references, bridge networks — use ±1% or better; general-purpose work is fine at ±5%.

Power rating

A resistor's value says how it behaves; its power rating says how much heat it can shed without failing. Common through-hole sizes are ¼ W, ½ W, 1 W, and up. The working rule: compute the real dissipation with P = I²R or P = V²/R, then pick a rating with margin — at least double is good practice.

Worked example

A 100 Ω resistor carries 200 mA. Which rating do you buy?

P = I²R = (0.2)² × 100 = 0.04 × 100 = 4 W
¼ W part → burns · 1 W part → burns · buy 5 W (or larger) with margin

Beyond fixed resistors

  • Potentiometers — three-terminal adjustable resistors; a wiper taps a fraction of the total. Volume knobs and position sensors.
  • Thermistors & RTDs — resistance changes with temperature; the basis of most temperature probes.
  • Strain gauges — a thin foil grid whose resistance rises a tiny amount when stretched. Bond one to a wing spar and resistance becomes a measure of structural load (lesson 6 shows how to read that tiny change).
Aero note

Aerospace test rigs are covered in resistive sensors: strain gauges on airframes, RTDs in engine instrumentation, potentiometer-style position transducers on control surfaces. "A resistance that varies with the physical world" is half of aircraft instrumentation.

Lesson 03

Series circuits

Components in series form a single path. One rule about current and one about voltage give you everything else.

One path, one current

Two components are in series when the same current has no choice but to flow through both — the tail of one connects to the head of the next with no branch in between. Because charge cannot pile up or leak out, the current is identical at every point in a series loop. Measure it anywhere; you get the same number.

Resistance adds

Each series resistor adds its own opposition, so the equivalent resistance is a plain sum — always bigger than the largest single resistor:

Req = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + …

Voltage drops

As current pushes through each resistor it gives up energy, so the voltage drops across each one by V = I × R. Around the whole loop the drops must account for exactly what the source supplies — this is Kirchhoff's voltage law, which lesson 6 states formally:

Vsource = V₁ + V₂ + V₃

Two consequences worth internalizing: the largest resistor drops the most voltage (same I, bigger R), and if the path breaks anywhere — a blown bulb, a broken wire — current stops everywhere, because there is no other route.

Worked example

2 kΩ, 4 kΩ, and 6 kΩ in series across a 24 V supply. Find the current and each drop.

Req = 2k + 4k + 6k = 12 kΩ
I = V / Req = 24 / 12000 = 2 mA
V₁ = 0.002 × 2000 = 4 V · V₂ = 0.002 × 4000 = 8 V · V₃ = 0.002 × 6000 = 12 V
Check: 4 + 8 + 12 = 24 V ✓

The voltage divider

Two series resistors are the standard trick for producing a smaller voltage from a bigger one. The output is tapped between them, and the fraction is just a resistance ratio — no current calculation needed:

Vout = Vin × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)
Worked example

Divide a 9 V supply down for a sensor using R₁ = 6 kΩ and R₂ = 3 kΩ.

Vout = 9 × 3000 / (6000 + 3000) = 9 × ⅓ = 3 V

Caution from practice: the formula assumes almost nothing draws current from the tap. A heavy load on Vout effectively adds itself in parallel with R₂ and drags the voltage down.

Aero note

Classic fuel-quantity systems are voltage dividers: the float arm drives a variable resistor, and the gauge reads the tapped voltage. A potentiometer is a voltage divider you can turn — trim position sensors work the same way.

Lesson 04

Parallel circuits

Components in parallel share the same two nodes. Voltage is shared, current divides, and total resistance always shrinks.

Same voltage, divided current

Components are in parallel when both of their terminals connect to the same pair of nodes, giving current multiple routes. Because each branch spans the same two points, every parallel branch sees the same voltage. The source current splits among the branches — each branch draws I = V / R independently — and the branch currents add back together at the nodes.

Equivalent resistance

More paths means less total opposition, so the equivalent of a parallel group is always smaller than its smallest member. The general rule adds the reciprocals:

1 / Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + …

Two shortcuts cover most real work:

  • Exactly two resistors — product over sum: R eq = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂). Example: 6 kΩ ‖ 3 kΩ = 18M/9k = 2 kΩ.
  • N equal resistors — divide by N: three 330 Ω in parallel make 110 Ω.
Worked example

A 12 V supply feeds a 4 Ω branch and a 6 Ω branch in parallel. Find the branch currents, total current, and Req.

I₁ = 12 / 4 = 3 A · I₂ = 12 / 6 = 2 A · Itotal = 5 A
Req = V / Itotal = 12 / 5 = 2.4 Ω
Check by product over sum: 4×6 / (4+6) = 24/10 = 2.4 Ω ✓

The current divider

When a known current reaches a two-branch split, it divides in inverse proportion to resistance — the smaller resistor takes the larger share. For two branches, the current through R₁ is:

I₁ = Itotal × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

Note the flip: it is the other branch's resistance on top. Sanity-check every answer with "the small resistor hogs the current."

Aero note

Every load on an aircraft bus — radios, lights, pumps — hangs in parallel, so each sees full bus voltage and any one can fail without silencing the rest. Redundant flight-critical systems take the same idea up a level: parallel paths so no single break is fatal.

Lesson 05

Series–parallel networks

Real circuits mix both configurations. The skill is seeing which is which, then collapsing the network one step at a time.

The reduction method

  1. Identify by nodes, not by drawing. Two resistors are in series only if the same current must pass through both (no branch between them); in parallel only if they connect the same two nodes. Redraw the circuit if the geometry is confusing — position on the page means nothing.
  2. Collapse the innermost group first — the combination farthest from the source — and replace it with one equivalent resistor.
  3. Repeat until one Req remains, then find the total current with Ohm's law.
  4. Expand back out to find individual voltages and currents, using voltage drops for series parts and shared voltage for parallel parts.
Worked example — the full solve

For the circuit above: total current, then every voltage and branch current.

Step 1 — collapse the pair: R₂ ‖ R₃ = 6×3/(6+3) = 18/9 = 2 Ω
Step 2 — now a plain series loop: Req = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω
Step 3 — total current: I = 12 / 6 = 2 A
Step 4 — expand: V across R₁ = 2 × 4 = 8 V; across the pair = 2 × 2 = 4 V (check: 8+4 = 12 ✓)
Step 5 — branch currents: I₂ = 4/6 = 0.667 A · I₃ = 4/3 = 1.333 A (sum = 2 A ✓)

Every solve carries its own error-checking: series drops must sum to the source, branch currents must sum to the total. Use both, every time.

Lesson 06 · Step up

Kirchhoff's laws & the Wheatstone bridge

The series and parallel rules you've been using are special cases of two laws that solve any circuit — and power the instrument bridge you'll meet throughout aerospace test engineering.

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)

Charge cannot accumulate at a junction, so the current flowing into any node equals the current flowing out. That's it — conservation of charge, applied locally. It is why parallel branch currents must add up to the supply current.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

Walk any closed loop in a circuit and the algebraic sum of the voltage changes is zero — every rise through a source is matched by drops across components before you return to your starting point. "Algebraic" is the operative word: pick a direction around the loop, count rises positive and drops negative, and the books must balance.

Σ Vloop = 0  (rises +, drops −)

For a single loop this is just "drops sum to the source," which you used in lesson 3. Its real power is on circuits with several loops and several sources, where series/parallel shortcuts break down: write KVL around each loop, KCL at each node, and solve the resulting equations.

Worked example

A loop contains a 12 V source and three resistors. Two of the drops measure 5 V and 4 V. What is the third?

+12 − 5 − 4 − V₃ = 0 → V₃ = 3 V

The Wheatstone bridge

The bridge is four resistors in a diamond with a sensitive meter across the middle. Each side of the diamond is a voltage divider; the meter compares their tapped voltages. When the ratios match, the taps sit at the same potential and the meter reads zero — the bridge is balanced:

R₁ / R₂ = R₃ / R₄ ⇒ V across the meter = 0

To measure an unknown resistance, put it in the R₄ position, adjust a known resistor until the meter nulls, and solve R₄ = R₂ × R₃ / R₁. Better still, the bridge converts a tiny change in one arm into a measurable meter voltage — which is exactly what strain measurement needs.

Worked example

R₁ = 1 kΩ, R₂ = 2 kΩ, R₃ = 1.5 kΩ. What value of R₄ balances the bridge?

R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄ → R₄ = R₂ × R₃ / R₁ = 2000 × 1500 / 1000 = 3 kΩ
Aero note

Bond a strain gauge to a wing spar and wire it as one bridge arm. Under load the gauge stretches, its resistance shifts a fraction of a percent, and the unbalanced bridge outputs a voltage proportional to strain. Structural test rigs, load cells, and pressure transducers are Wheatstone bridges all the way down.

Lesson 07 · Step up

Capacitors, inductors & RC transients

Resistors only dissipate. The other two passive components store energy — and storage introduces time into circuit behavior.

Capacitors

A capacitor is two conductive plates separated by an insulator. Push charge onto it and a voltage builds: Q = C × V, where capacitance C is measured in farads (F) — practical parts run from picofarads to millifarads. A capacitor passes current only while its voltage is changing; once charged to the supply voltage, current stops. It stores energy in an electric field and blocks DC while coupling changing signals.

Combination rules are the reverse of resistors: parallel capacitors add (more plate area), while series capacitors combine by reciprocals.

Inductors

An inductor is a coil of wire, with inductance L measured in henries (H). It stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes changes in current — current through an inductor cannot jump instantly, just as voltage across a capacitor cannot. Inductors combine like resistors: series adds, parallel by reciprocals. You'll meet them in filters, motors, relays, and transformers.

The RC time constant

Put a resistor in series with a capacitor and close the switch. The capacitor charges quickly at first, then ever more slowly as its rising voltage cuts the charging current. The pace of the whole process is set by one number, the time constant:

τ = R × C (seconds = ohms × farads)

After one time constant the capacitor has reached about 63% of the supply voltage; each further τ closes 63% of the remaining gap. By 5τ it is above 99% — "fully charged" for engineering purposes. Discharging runs the same curve in reverse: down to 37% after one τ.

Worked example

A 10 kΩ resistor charges a 100 µF capacitor from a 28 V bus. How long to 63%? To "full"?

τ = R × C = 10 000 × 0.0001 = 1 s → 63% (≈17.6 V) after 1 s
5τ = 5 s to >99% of 28 V

Switch-on and steady state

Two limiting cases carry you through most transient reasoning without any calculus:

  • At the instant of switch-on, an uncharged capacitor behaves like a short circuit (a plain wire); a long time later, like an open circuit (a break).
  • An inductor is the mirror image: open at the first instant, short in steady state.
Aero note

Aircraft AC power runs at 400 Hz rather than the ground grid's 50/60 Hz because transformer and motor cores can be far smaller and lighter at higher frequency — and weight is everything in the air. RC time constants set the response lag in sensor filters, and capacitor banks ride avionics through momentary bus transfers.
